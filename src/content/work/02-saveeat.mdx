<Section id="question" label="The question">

<Lede text={frontmatter.question} />

TooGoodToGo works on a straightforward trade, in which a shop has food it cannot sell
and you get it cheaply, but in return you do not get to choose what it is.

The discount, in that arrangement, is what makes the surprise acceptable.

Korean dining has moved towards health-conscious eating, where people choose a meal on
what is in it rather than on what it costs.

Once the buyer starts caring about ingredients, however, a mystery bag stops being a
bargain and becomes a risk, so this project set out to keep the surplus-food model while
taking that risk back out of it.

<Section id="onboarding" label="Onboarding and dietary preference">

<div class="prose">

The flow asks two things before it shows any food, namely what kind of user you are and
what you eat, and the second of those is the decision the whole product rests on.

Every screen before the first bag is a screen somebody can leave on, and most
marketplaces earn the right to ask by showing you something first, which is exactly what
this one cannot do.

If a health-led buyer's first sight is a wall of unlabelled mystery bags, the model has
already failed for them, and no amount of filtering afterwards undoes it.

</div>

<Section id="process" label="Process and contribution">

<div class="prose">

The team worked in weekly cycles that ran in the same order each time, moving from **research** to **feature** and then to **screen**.

</div>

<Method>

<Step n="01" title="Research">
Ground rules, background and target user, which is where the health-led framing was
settled.
</Step>

<Step n="02" title="Feature">
Key features, where AI would and would not be applied, and a functional
specification.
</Step>

<Step n="03" title="Screen">
Wireframes and then screens, with each member building MVP screens with AI assistance
into a shared Figma file, after which mine, the merchant pages and the impact UI, became
the basis for the whole flow.
</Step>

</Method>

<Section id="stands" label="Where it stands">

<div class="prose">

There are no usage numbers yet, because nothing has been put in front of a user, so what
the project is worth at this stage is the specificity of its proposal.

Surplus-food marketplaces are well established in places where a discount is the draw,
but whether the model survives in a market that chooses a meal on what is in it has not
really been answered.

The mechanism is small and testable: move the dietary question in front of the food,
carry it through every screen, and a mystery bag becomes a bounded one.

As it stands there is a full buyer flow and a merchant side designed, a working
prototype, and a design system being retrofitted rather than planned, with nothing yet
shipped to users.

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="reflections" label="Reflections">

<div class="prose">

I had assumed the thinking would happen while drawing, and it did not.

Writing the functional specification first meant the screen work was about presenting
decisions that had already been argued rather than making them with a mouse. The screens
got faster and the arguments got shorter.

Choosing B was the decision I am most sure of and also the one that felt least like
designing, because the instinct in that moment is to invent the interaction.

The better answer was to borrow one the market already knows, and spend the attention we
saved on the genuinely unfamiliar part, being asked what you eat before you are shown any
food.

The honest regret is the design system, since colours, spacing and components were
settled screen by screen and reconciled afterwards, which leaves behind decisions that
nobody can explain later.

Next time the system comes after the first flow is understood and before the rest is
drawn, not at the end.

</div>

</Section>
