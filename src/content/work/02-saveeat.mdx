<Section id="question" label="The question">

<p class="lede">{frontmatter.question}</p>

TooGoodToGo works on a straightforward trade. A shop has food it cannot sell, and you
get it cheaply on the understanding that you do not get to choose what it is. The
surprise is the point, and the discount is what makes the surprise acceptable.

Korean dining has moved towards health-conscious eating, where people often choose a meal
on what is in it rather than on what it costs. That puts pressure on the part of the
model that usually does the work: if the buyer cares about ingredients, a mystery bag is
not a bargain, it is a risk. The project is an attempt to keep the surplus-food model
while removing that risk.

</Section>

<Section id="method" label="How the project ran">

<div class="prose">

Our team worked in weekly cycles and followed this process: **research**, then
**feature**, then **screen**.

</div>

<Method>

<Step n="01" title="Research">
Ground rules, background and target user. This is where the health-led framing was
settled, and it is the decision everything downstream depends on: it is why the product
cannot simply copy the mystery-bag mechanic.
</Step>

<Step n="02" title="Feature">
Key features, where AI would and would not be applied, and a functional specification.
Writing the spec before the screens meant the later screen work was about how to present
decisions that had already been argued, rather than making them by drawing.
</Step>

<Step n="03" title="Screen">
Wireframes, then screens. Each member built MVP screens with AI assistance and brought
them into a shared Figma file. Mine, the merchant pages and the impact UI, were taken as
the basis for completing the whole flow.
</Step>

</Method>

</Section>

<Section id="onboarding" label="Onboarding">

<div class="prose">

Onboarding is where a surplus-food app either sets expectations about uncertainty or
fails to, so it is the right place to start when the open question is whether buyers
will accept not choosing. The flow asks two things before it shows any food: what kind
of user you are, and what you eat.

</div>

<Section id="app" label="The main flow">

<div class="prose">

The buyer side runs from a home feed and a map of nearby bags, into a store, into a
pickup pass. Filters and dietary tags carry the onboarding answers forward, so the
preference collected at the start does work on every subsequent screen rather than
sitting in a profile.

<Section id="ab" label="The A/B comparison">

<div class="prose">

The map view, where you see what is available near you, was drawn twice and the two
versions put side by side. The question underneath them is how the screen should split
between the map and the list of bags, and that decision is not obvious: the map is what
tells you a pickup is walkable, and the list is what tells you whether the food is worth
walking for.

</div>

<Screens
  cap={2}
  items={[
    ["/images/saveeat/ab-variant-a.jpg", "A. A fixed split. The map stops partway down and the list sits below it, so three bags read in full without any interaction.", "650/1806"],
    ["/images/saveeat/ab-variant-b.jpg", "B, the one we went with. A draggable sheet over a taller map. More of the area is visible, and an extra pin appears with it, but the list is cut to two and a half cards.", "650/1804"],
  ]}
/>

<div class="prose">

Everything else is held constant: the same category chips over the map, the same cards,
the same four-item bottom navigation. The variable is the split, and what changes with it
is which of the two questions the screen answers first.

We went with B, and the argument that settled it was not really about the screen. It is
the layout KakaoMap uses, and KakaoMap is how most people in this market already look for
something near them. Choosing it means the sheet is a gesture they have used before, so
none of their attention goes on working out how the map behaves. A surplus-food app is
already asking people to accept some uncertainty about the food. The interface is not the
place to add more.

</div>

</Section>
