<Section id="question" label="The question">

<p class="lede">{frontmatter.question}</p>

TooGoodToGo works on a straightforward trade: a shop has food it cannot sell, you get
it cheaply, and in return you do not get to choose what it is.

The discount is what makes the surprise acceptable.

Korean dining has moved towards health-conscious eating, where people choose a meal on
what is in it rather than on what it costs.

If the buyer cares about ingredients, a mystery bag is not a bargain, it is a risk.
This project keeps the surplus-food model and removes that risk.

<Section id="onboarding" label="Onboarding and dietary preference">

<div class="prose">

The flow asks two things before it shows any food: what kind of user you are, and what
you eat. The second is the decision the whole product rests on.

Every screen before the first bag is a screen somebody can leave on, and most
marketplaces earn the right to ask by showing you something first. This one cannot.

If a health-led buyer's first sight is a wall of unlabelled mystery bags, the model has
already failed for them, and no amount of filtering afterwards undoes it.

</div>

Weekly cycles: **research**, then **feature**, then **screen**.

</div>

<Section id="stands" label="Where it stands">

<div class="prose">

No usage numbers, because nothing has been put in front of a user yet. What the
project is worth is the specificity of the proposal.

Surplus-food marketplaces are well established where a discount is the draw. Whether the
model survives in a market that chooses a meal on what is in it has not been answered.

The mechanism is small and testable: move the dietary question in front of the food,
carry it through every screen, and a mystery bag becomes a bounded one.

A full buyer flow and a merchant side designed, a working prototype, a design system
being retrofitted rather than planned. Nothing shipped to users.

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="reflections" label="Reflections">

<div class="prose">

I had assumed the thinking happened while drawing. It did not.

Writing the functional specification first meant the screen work was about presenting
decisions that had already been argued rather than making them with a mouse. The screens
got faster and the arguments got shorter.

Choosing B was the decision I am most sure of and the one that felt least like designing.
The instinct is to invent the interaction.

The better answer was to borrow one the market already knows, and spend the attention we
saved on the genuinely unfamiliar part, being asked what you eat before you are shown any
food.

The honest regret is the design system. Colours, spacing and components were settled
screen by screen and reconciled afterwards, which leaves decisions nobody can explain
later.

Next time the system comes after the first flow is understood and before the rest is
drawn, not at the end.

</div>

</Section>
