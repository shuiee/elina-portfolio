<Section id="question" label="The question">

<p class="lede">{frontmatter.question}</p>

TooGoodToGo works on a straightforward trade. A shop has food it cannot sell, and you
get it cheaply on the understanding that you do not get to choose what it is. The
surprise is the point, and the discount is what makes the surprise acceptable.

Korean dining has moved towards health-conscious eating, where people often choose a meal
on what is in it rather than on what it costs. That puts pressure on the part of the
model that usually does the work: if the buyer cares about ingredients, a mystery bag is
not a bargain, it is a risk. The project is an attempt to keep the surplus-food model
while removing that risk.

<Section id="diet" label="Asking what you eat first">

<div class="prose">

The flow asks two things before it shows any food: what kind of user you are, and what
you eat. The second one is the decision the whole product rests on.

Putting a question in front of the value is the expensive place to put it. Every screen
before the first bag is a screen somebody can leave on, and most marketplaces earn the
right to ask by showing you something first. This one cannot. If the first thing a
health-led buyer sees is a wall of unlabelled mystery bags, the model has already failed
for them, and no amount of filtering afterwards undoes that first impression. Asking
early is what turns an unbounded surprise into a bounded one.

</div>

<Section id="split" label="The map or the list">

<div class="prose">

The map view, where you see what is available near you, was drawn twice and the two
versions put side by side. The question underneath them is how the screen should split
between the map and the list of bags, and that decision is not obvious: the map is what
tells you a pickup is walkable, and the list is what tells you whether the food is worth
walking for.

</div>

<Section id="team" label="How the team worked">

<div class="prose">

Our team worked in weekly cycles and followed this process: **research**, then
**feature**, then **screen**.

</div>

<Method>

<Step n="01" title="Research">
Ground rules, background and target user. This is where the health-led framing was
settled, which is why the product cannot simply copy the mystery-bag mechanic.
</Step>

<Step n="02" title="Feature">
Key features, where AI would and would not be applied, and a functional specification.
Writing the spec before the screens meant the later screen work was about how to present
decisions that had already been argued, rather than making them by drawing.
</Step>

<Step n="03" title="Screen">
Wireframes, then screens. Each member built MVP screens with AI assistance and brought
them into a shared Figma file. Mine, the merchant pages and the impact UI, were taken as
the basis for completing the whole flow.
</Step>

</Method>

</Section>

<Section id="stands" label="Where it stands">

<div class="prose">

There are no usage numbers to report, because nothing has been put in front of a user
yet. What the project is worth is the specificity of the proposal. Surplus-food
marketplaces are well established where a discount is the draw; whether the model
survives in a market that chooses a meal on what is in it has not really been answered,
and this is an answer rather than an argument about one. The mechanism is small and
testable: move the dietary question in front of the food, carry it through every
subsequent screen, and a mystery bag becomes a bounded one.

Where the project is: a full buyer flow and a merchant side designed, a working
prototype, a design system being retrofitted rather than planned. Nothing yet shipped
to users.

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="reflection" label="What I took from it">

<div class="prose">

I had assumed the thinking happened while drawing. It did not. The cycle that worked was
research, then feature, then screen, and writing the functional specification first meant
the screen work was about presenting decisions that had already been argued rather than
making them with a mouse. The screens got faster and the arguments got shorter.

Choosing B was the decision I am most sure of and the one that felt least like designing.
The instinct is to invent the interaction. The better answer was to borrow one the market
already knows, and spend the attention we saved on the part of the product that is
genuinely unfamiliar, which is being asked what you eat before you are shown any food.

The honest regret is the design system. Colours, spacing and components were settled
screen by screen and reconciled afterwards, which is slower than it sounds and leaves
decisions that nobody can explain later. On the next one the system comes after the first
flow is understood and before the rest of it is drawn, not at the end.

</div>

</Section>
