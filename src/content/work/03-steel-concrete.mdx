<Section id="question" label="The question">

<Lede text={frontmatter.question} />

Reinforced concrete is what nuclear buildings are normally made of. Steel bars are tied
into a cage, concrete is poured around them, and the two work together.

A reactor building has to do more than stand up. It has to shield what is inside it and
survive being struck from outside, and the codes written for that push its walls and
slabs thick.

Thick sections need dense reinforcement. Tying that cage and inspecting it becomes the
slow step on a site where every step is inspected and every pour has to cure.

Steel concrete turns the panel inside out. Two steel plates form the faces, the concrete
goes between them, and the plates do the work of the formwork and the reinforcement at
once.

That is what makes it modular, because a panel built that way can be fabricated off site
and lifted into place. It is not hypothetical either: SC is already used in third
generation plants, including the containment internal structures and shield building of
the AP1000.

The catch is regulatory. A system that different needs its own code route, and this
project walks one slab down that route to see whether it arrives.

</Section>

<Section id="scope" label="One slab, three tests">

<div class="prose">

Calling a system modular proves nothing on its own. The claim worth testing is that an SC
slab behaves like the RC slab it replaces, under the codes that govern nuclear
construction.

So the scope came down to a single ten by ten metre slab inside a two storey reference
building, and three things done to it.

</div>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Modelled and verified RC and SC slabs under static loading to Eurocode 2, AISC N690-24 and SCI P414</li>
  <li>Calibrated stiffness and mass in Mathcad so the digital RC model would behave like a SteelBrick system</li>
  <li>Evaluated both systems under an aircraft impact scenario, testing local failure limits</li>
</ul>

</Section>

<Section id="method" label="Methodology">

<div class="prose">

Two tracks, run separately. One asks whether the slab stands up under everyday loading,
the other asks what happens when something hits it.

<div class="prose">

Both tracks loop. A failed deflection check sends the static route back to the
calculations rather than forward, and a failed impact limit does the same, which is the
part of the diagram that reflects how the work actually went.

</div>

<Section id="model" label="Building and converting the model">

<div class="prose">

The model only had to be as detailed as the question required: a slab that behaves like
SteelBrick in mass and stiffness, without every plate and tie drawn in.

I built the two storey ten by ten metre reinforced concrete building in SAP2000 and
verified its bending and shear by hand against Eurocode 2, so the baseline was known to
be right before anything was changed.

Converting it is where the interesting constraint sits. SteelBrick differs from an RC
slab in two ways that matter to a model: it is composite steel panels with concrete
infill, and it is stiffer in one direction than the other.

AISC N690 clause N9.2.3 settles how to represent that. Even though the system is full of
steel, its global behaviour is carried by concrete-based material inputs, so the slab
stays a concrete slab in the model and the steel arrives as modified stiffness.

I derived a slab thickness that matched SteelBrick's mass, calculated the effective
flexural and shear stiffness in each direction, and applied those as directional
modifiers in SAP2000.

Then I compared deflection and stiffness trends against the reference SteelBrick
behaviour until they matched within a reasonable range, and applied the result to the
building model.

The last step is the one I would defend hardest. I moved every stiffness modifier by
about ten per cent either way, to confirm that any difference between the RC and
SteelBrick options came from the systems themselves rather than from how I had modelled
them.

</div>
