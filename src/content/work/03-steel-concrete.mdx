<Section id="question" label="The question">

<Lede text={frontmatter.question} />

Nuclear buildings are normally made of reinforced concrete, which is steel bars tied
into a cage with concrete poured around them.

The role of a reactor building extends further than just standing up, because it also
has to shield what is inside it and survive being struck from the outside, and the
structural codes written for that mean its walls and slabs become extremely thick. Thick
sections need dense reinforcement, which makes tying and inspecting that cage the slow
step on a site where every stage has to be signed off and every pour has to cure.

Steel concrete, usually shortened to SC, turns the panel inside out: two steel plates
form the faces and the concrete goes between them, allowing the plates to do the work of
the formwork and the reinforcement simultaneously. That is what makes the system
modular, because a panel built this way can be fabricated off site and transported into
place. While it is still a relatively new material, SC is already in use in third
generation plants, including the containment internal structures and the shield building
of the AP1000.

The application of a novel material brings its own codes and regulations with it,
however, and working through those is what this project set out to do. I took a single
ten by ten metre slab inside a two storey reference building and carried it down that
route three times over: modelling and verifying both the RC and the SC versions under
static loading to Eurocode 2, AISC N690-24 and SCI P414; calibrating stiffness and mass
in Mathcad so that the digital RC model would behave like a SteelBrick system; and
finally assessing both systems under an aircraft impact scenario to find where each one
fails locally.

</Section>

<Section id="model" label="Building and converting the model">

<div class="prose">

The model only had to be as detailed as the question required: a slab that behaves like
SteelBrick in mass and stiffness, without every plate and tie drawn in.

I built the two storey ten by ten metre reinforced concrete building in SAP2000 and
verified its bending and shear by hand against Eurocode 2, so the baseline was known to
be right before anything was changed.

<div class="prose">

Converting it is where the interesting constraint sits, because SteelBrick differs from
an RC slab in two ways that matter to a model: it is composite steel panels with
concrete infill, and it is stiffer in one direction than it is in the other.

AISC N690 clause N9.2.3 settles how that should be represented, because even though the
system is full of steel its global behaviour is carried by concrete-based material
inputs, which means the slab stays a concrete slab in the model and the steel arrives
instead as modified stiffness.

</div>

<div class="prose">

I derived a slab thickness that matched SteelBrick's mass, calculated the effective
flexural and shear stiffness in each direction, and applied those as directional
modifiers in SAP2000.

Then I compared deflection and stiffness trends against the reference SteelBrick
behaviour until they matched within a reasonable range, and applied the result to the
building model.

The last step is the one I would defend hardest, which was to move every stiffness
modifier by about ten per cent in either direction, confirming that any difference
between the RC and the SteelBrick options came from the systems themselves rather than
from the way I had modelled them.

</div>
