<Section id="question" label="The question">

<p class="lede">{frontmatter.question}</p>

Over six million people in London rely on buses every day, and I kept seeing
passengers hesitate or board the wrong one because the signs were hard to read.
Coming from Korea, where bus signage is denser and easier to scan, the contrast
was hard to ignore.

A sign has a fixed amount of room and a reader who is moving, often in bad light,
often at a distance. Every question below is a version of the same one: how much
can you ask of that reader before they stop being able to answer.

</Section>

<Section id="route" label="How it ran">

<div class="prose">

The project ran as a route, so the page runs as one too. Each stop is a stage of
the work in the order it happened, and each one had to produce something
measurable before the next could start.

</div>

<Route label="The route">

<Stop label="Stop 01" title="Background">

Buses are the part of the network people use without planning, which is exactly
why the signage has to work at a glance. London's does not, reliably. Korean bus
signage carries more information in the same space and is easier to scan, and
that difference is what made me want to measure rather than assume.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 02" title="Vision">

Work out how information is actually perceived in a transport environment, and
whether design choices like LED against printed, or a change of orientation,
make a sign measurably clearer rather than just different.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 03" title="Problem framing">

A literature review turned up four recurring failures. Human vision leans on
quick fixations and pre-attentive cues, and none of these support that.

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Unclear information hierarchy</li>
  <li>Inconsistent contrast and lighting</li>
  <li>Fonts that lose clarity when digitised</li>
  <li>LED scrolling that outruns reading</li>
</ul>

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 04" title="Measuring luminance and illuminance">

Turning readability into something measurable. I compared a printed sign against
an LED sign under controlled conditions at UCL's PEARL facility, recording how
much light each sign emits from its white letters and black ground, and how much
light falls onto it.

The test word was chosen before the test day rather than during it: a single word
of at least seven characters with both straight and curved letters, so edges and
curves were measured under the same conditions.

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Test word: Shoreditch 388</li>
  <li>8 fixed illuminance points, mirrored on both signs</li>
  <li>5 distances, 0.5 to 2.5 m</li>
  <li>Lighting passport to control for ambient light</li>
  <li>Black cloth under the LED screen to kill reflections</li>
</ul>

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 05" title="Evaluating fonts with MATLAB">

Translating legibility into geometry and pixel data. A script measured area,
eccentricity and circularity for every character, then pulled a pixel-intensity
profile along a horizontal line through the text, which shows how sharply
brightness moves from ground to letter edge.

The point was never a perfect threshold. It was whether Johnston 100 behaves like
a clinical-clarity font or a decorative one when you look only at geometry, which
is why it is benchmarked against the face used in visual-acuity testing.

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Johnston 100 Medium</li>
  <li>Arial</li>
  <li>Times New Roman</li>
  <li>Motorway</li>
  <li>Apple Chancery</li>
  <li>Benchmarked against Optician Sans</li>
</ul>

Johnston 100 held the most stable geometry under pixelation. Serif and decorative
faces degraded quickly with distance on LED, and the intensity profiles confirmed
soft edges beyond about two metres.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 06" title="Passenger opinion">

A Qualtrics survey on what people want shown, and how much they can actually read
off a scrolling sign.

The part worth keeping is the marking scheme. Someone who recalls "Shoredich"
will still board the right bus, so scoring recall as right or wrong would have
thrown away the truth. I built fictitious station names, showed them at three
scroll intervals, and graded what people typed back.

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Threshold: 5 of 9 letters correct</li>
  <li>6 of 9 in the right position</li>
  <li>Intervals 1.5 s, 3 s, 4.5 s</li>
  <li>Graded incorrect, correct enough, perfect</li>
</ul>

English also costs more horizontal space than Korean does, which is a large part
of why a Korean sign can carry more without losing legibility. That constraint
shaped the design variations rather than sitting in the background.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 07" title="What passengers said">

<ul class="figures">
  <li><b>81%</b><span>wanted main stations shown on bus signs</span></li>
  <li><b>84.2%</b><span>wanted occupancy for the next bus</span></li>
  <li><b>82.2%</b><span>ranked Design 1 the clearest occupancy symbol</span></li>
  <li><b>3 s</b><span>the scroll interval with the most reliable recall</span></li>
</ul>

Passengers valued simple, high-contrast cues confirming destination and occupancy
at a glance, above decorative clarity.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Terminus" title="Design implications">

Four rules came out of it, each traceable to a specific experiment rather than to
taste.

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Prioritise luminance contrast, not colour accents</li>
  <li>Use fonts that hold their shape under pixelation</li>
  <li>Keep motion predictable and scrolling short</li>
  <li>Keep decision-critical information static</li>
</ul>

What I would test next: varied lighting and viewing angles, LCD as well as LED,
sharper visual-acuity metrics, and how much information a sign can carry before
clarity starts to fall away.

</Stop>

</Route>
