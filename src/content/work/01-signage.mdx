them rests on an opinion about what looks clearer. Each is traceable to a measurement.

If a passenger remembers "Shoredich" instead of "Shoreditch" they have understood the
sign and will almost certainly board the right bus. Marking that as a failure would
have thrown away the truth, so I created fictitious station names, assigned them to
different scrolling intervals, and graded what participants typed back as incorrect,
correct enough, or perfect.

<Section id="route" label="The route">

<Route>

<Stop label="Stop 01" title="Background">

In London, over six million people rely on buses every day, yet I often saw
passengers hesitating or boarding the wrong one because the signs were hard to
read. Coming from Korea, where bus signage is more information-rich and easier
to scan, this contrast stood out even more. It made me question why a city as
connected as London still struggles with such a fundamental part of wayfinding,
and how clearer visual systems could genuinely improve the passenger experience.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 02" title="Vision">

My objective was to explore how information is perceived in the transport
environment, and how better design choices, for example LED against printed signs
or using different orientations, can make bus signs clearer, friendlier, more
informative, and ultimately improve traveller experience.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 03" title="Problem framing" href="#framing">

A literature review into why passengers struggle with bus signs. Human vision

<Section id="framing" label="Problem framing">

<div class="prose">

I conducted a literature review to understand why passengers struggle with bus
signs. Four failures came up repeatedly.

</div>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Unclear information hierarchy</li>
  <li>Inconsistent contrast and lighting</li>
  <li>Fonts that change clarity when digitised</li>
  <li>LED scrolling systems that can be difficult to read</li>
</ul>

<div class="prose">

Human vision depends heavily on quick fixations and pre-attentive cues, which
current signage does not support.

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="light" label="Measuring luminance and illuminance">

<div class="prose">

Turning readability into something measurable. I compared a real printed sign
against an LED sign under controlled conditions at UCL's PEARL facility, measuring
how much light each sign emits from its white letters and black background, and
how much light falls onto each sign.

</div>

<Figure
  src="/images/signage/pearl-rig.jpg"
  ratio="1800/471"
  caption="The two set-ups at PEARL: a real double-decker with its printed blind, and an LED sign on a stand with black cloth beneath it to cut reflections."
/>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Lighting passport to control ambient light and colour temperature</li>
  <li>8 fixed illuminance points, mirrored on the LED sign</li>
  <li>Luminance sampled on fixed black and white points</li>
  <li>Five distances, 0.5 to 2.5 m, repeated to smooth out hand-held noise</li>
  <li>Black cloth under the LED screen to reduce reflections</li>
</ul>

<div class="prose">

Three things came out of the readings. LED signs consistently showed higher luminance
contrast than printed ones. Pixelation softened curved letters more than straight-edged
ones. And readability fell away sharply once ambient light passed roughly 500 lux, which
is an ordinary bright afternoon rather than an edge case.

So LED is not simply better. It improves legibility where contrast is maximised and
curvature-heavy fonts are handled carefully, and loses that advantage as soon as the
ambient light gets bright enough.

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="fonts" label="Evaluating fonts with MATLAB">

<div class="prose">

The question this had to answer was narrow: does the LED version make a sharper, clearer
sign, or do distance and pixelation soften the edges too much compared with print?

Using MATLAB's regionprops, I calculated area, eccentricity and circularity for every
character in Shoreditch 388, then drew a horizontal line through the text to extract a
pixel-intensity profile, which shows how brightness moves from a dark background to a
bright letter edge and back. A crisp edge reads as a square wave. A soft one reads as a
slope.

</div>

<Figure
  src="/images/signage/matlab-profiles.jpg"
  ratio="1800/1268"
  caption="The sampling line drawn through Shoreditch 388, and the intensity profiles it produced for printed and LED signs at close and far distances. Square-shouldered pulses are sharp letter edges; the rounded ones are edges that have started to dissolve."
/>

<div class="prose">

The aim was to remove subjectivity. Instead of saying one sign looks clearer, I wanted a
repeatable workflow a transport operator could run on a new font or a new sign. Johnston
100 is benchmarked against Optician Sans, the face used in clinical visual-acuity
testing, so the comparison has a reference point outside transport.

</div>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Johnston 100 Medium</li>
  <li>Arial</li>
  <li>Times New Roman</li>
  <li>Motorway</li>
  <li>Apple Chancery</li>
  <li>Benchmarked against Optician Sans</li>
</ul>

<div class="prose">

Johnston 100 showed the most stable geometry under pixelation. Serif and
decorative fonts degraded quickly with distance on LED screens, and the
pixel-intensity profiles confirmed soft edges beyond about two metres.

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="survey" label="Passenger opinion">

<div class="prose">

Having quantified light and font behaviour, the last step was to find out how
people actually prefer information to be shown, and how much they can realistically
read from a scrolling LED sign. I sketched the front and side sign concepts at the
angles passengers really see a double-decker from, then built a Qualtrics survey
around four questions: whether current placements are clear, whether people want
occupancy and via stations, which symbol communicates occupancy best, and what
scrolling interval allows a station name to be recalled.

</div>

<Figure
  src="/images/signage/occupancy-symbols.jpg"
  ratio="1600/826"
  caption="Three ways of saying how full the bus is: filled human figures, filled bus outlines, and faces. All three use the same green, amber and red, so what is being tested is the symbol rather than the colour."
/>

<div class="prose">

Letters in English take up more horizontal space than Korean characters do, which
is a large part of why a Korean bus sign carries more information without losing
legibility. So the limit on how much a London sign can say is typographic rather than
editorial.

The fourth question needed a stimulus rather than a picture to rank. Participants
watched a station name scroll past at 1.5, 3 or 4.5 seconds and typed back what they
recalled. The marking scheme at the top of this page is what graded them.

</div>

<Figure
  src="/images/signage/led-sign.jpg"
  ratio="1800/763"
  caption="The scrolling stimulus: a fictitious route, 459 Bertsklan, with occupancy and a via station underneath it."
/>

</Section>

<Section id="findings" label="Findings and future work">

<Findings
  items={[
    ["81%", "wanted main stations displayed on bus signs"],
    ["72.6%", "wanted occupancy information for the current bus"],
    ["84.2%", "wanted occupancy information for the next bus"],
    ["68.6%", "preferred the bus number on the left of the sign"],
    ["82.2%", "ranked Design 1 the clearest occupancy symbol"],
    ["3 s", "the scroll interval with the most reliable recall"],
  ]}
/>

<div class="prose">

Passengers valued simple, high-contrast cues confirming both destination and
occupancy at a glance, above decorative clarity. Four design rules came out of it,
each traceable to a specific experiment rather than to taste.

</div>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Prioritise luminance contrast, not colour accents</li>
  <li>Use fonts that retain clarity under pixelation</li>
  <li>Keep motion predictable and minimise total scrolling content</li>
  <li>Keep decision-critical information static</li>
</ul>

<div class="prose">

What this established: which of the tested faces hold their geometry under pixelation
and distance, how much a printed and an LED sign differ in luminance contrast and where
that advantage stops, what passengers say they want on a sign, and which scroll interval
produced the most reliable recall under test.

What it did not: whether any of the six concepts reduces boarding errors at a real stop.
The light readings came from a controlled facility, and the recall results from people
sitting at a browser rather than standing at a kerb in the rain. The rules are supported
by the measurements, but the signs themselves have not been put in front of a passenger
who was actually trying to catch a bus.

Looking ahead: testing under varied lighting and angles, assessing additional sign
types such as liquid-crystal displays, refining the visual-acuity metrics, and
studying how much information a sign can realistically carry before clarity starts
to fall away.

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="reflection" label="What I took from it">

<div class="prose">

The marking scheme is the part I would do again first. It looks like a measurement
decision and it is really a design decision: choosing to grade "Shoredich" as a success
is choosing to measure whether somebody catches their bus rather than whether they spell
the stop correctly.
Getting the instrument wrong would have produced clean numbers about the wrong thing.

Coming from Korea is what made the London signs visible to me at all, but noticing is
not a finding. The useful move was converting that impression into something a person
could argue with: English letterforms take more horizontal space than Korean ones, so
the difference in how much a sign carries is partly a typographic constraint rather than
a difference in ambition.

I had spent four years in structural engineering, where a code tells you what adequate
means before you start. Nothing here did. Deciding that 5 of 9 letters in 6 of 9
positions counted as good enough was the first time I had to write the threshold myself
and then defend it, and it is the habit I have carried into every design problem since.

What I would change: I measured perception carefully and never closed the loop. The next
version of this project starts by putting a redesigned sign at a real stop.

</div>

</Section>
