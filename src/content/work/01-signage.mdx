<Section id="route" label="The route">

<Lede text={frontmatter.question} />

<Route>

<Stop label="Stop 01" title="Background">

Over six million people rely on London buses every day, yet I kept seeing
passengers hesitate or board the wrong one because the signs were hard to read.
Coming from Korea, where bus signage is more information-rich and easier to
scan, the contrast was hard to ignore.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 02" title="Vision">

To find out how much a passenger takes in at a bus stop, and whether design
choices like LED against print, or a change of orientation, measurably improve
it.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 03" title="Problem framing" href="#framing">

A literature review into why passengers struggle with bus signs.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 04" title="Measuring luminance and illuminance" href="#light">

Turning readability into something measurable, under controlled conditions at
UCL's PEARL facility.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 05" title="Evaluating fonts with MATLAB" href="#fonts">

Translating legibility into geometry and pixel data, to see whether Johnston 100
behaves like a clinical-clarity font or a decorative one.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 06" title="Passenger opinion" href="#survey">

A survey on what people want shown, and how much they can actually read off a
scrolling sign.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Terminus" title="Findings and future work" href="#findings">

What the four experiments agreed on, and the four design rules that came out of
them.

</Stop>

</Route>

<Section id="framing" label="Problem framing">

<div class="prose">

Four failures came up repeatedly in the literature.

</div>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Unclear information hierarchy</li>
  <li>Inconsistent contrast and lighting</li>
  <li>Fonts that change clarity when digitised</li>
  <li>LED scrolling systems that can be difficult to read</li>
</ul>

<div class="prose">

Human vision depends on quick fixations and pre-attentive cues. Current signage
supports neither.

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="light" label="Measuring luminance and illuminance">

<div class="prose">

I compared a printed sign against an LED sign at UCL's PEARL facility, measuring
the light each emits from its white letters and black background, and the light
falling onto it.

The test word was fixed before the test day: Shoreditch 388, seven characters
with both straight and curved letters, so edges and curves met the same
conditions.

</div>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Lighting passport to control ambient light and colour temperature</li>
  <li>8 fixed illuminance points, mirrored on the LED sign</li>
  <li>Luminance sampled on fixed black and white points</li>
  <li>Five distances, 0.5 to 2.5 m, repeated to smooth out hand-held noise</li>
  <li>Black cloth under the LED screen to reduce reflections</li>
</ul>

</Section>

<Section id="fonts" label="Evaluating fonts with MATLAB">

<div class="prose">

Using MATLAB's regionprops, I calculated area, eccentricity and circularity for
every character in Shoreditch 388, then drew a horizontal line through the text
to extract a pixel-intensity profile: how brightness moves from dark background
to bright letter edge and back.

The aim was a repeatable workflow a transport operator could run on a new font,
not a judgment that one sign looks clearer. Optician Sans, the face used in
clinical visual-acuity testing, is the benchmark, so the comparison has a
reference point outside transport.

</div>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Johnston 100 Medium</li>
  <li>Arial</li>
  <li>Times New Roman</li>
  <li>Motorway</li>
  <li>Apple Chancery</li>
  <li>Benchmarked against Optician Sans</li>
</ul>

<div class="prose">

Johnston 100 showed the most stable geometry under pixelation. Serif and
decorative fonts degraded quickly with distance on LED screens, and the
pixel-intensity profiles confirmed soft edges beyond about two metres.

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="survey" label="Passenger opinion">

<div class="prose">

I sketched front and side sign concepts at the angles passengers really see a
double-decker from, then built a Qualtrics survey around four questions: whether
current placements are clear, whether people want occupancy and via stations,
which symbol reads as occupancy, and what scrolling interval allows a station
name to be recalled.

English letters take more horizontal space than Korean characters, which is much
of why a Korean bus sign carries more without losing legibility.

</div>

<div class="prose">

A passenger who remembers "Shoredich" instead of "Shoreditch" still boards the
right bus, so grading right or wrong would have thrown the truth away. I assigned
fictitious station names to different scrolling intervals and graded what
participants typed back against a marking scheme.

</div>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Intervals 1.5 s, 3 s, 4.5 s</li>
  <li>Threshold: 5 of 9 letters correct</li>
  <li>6 of 9 letters in the right position</li>
  <li>Graded incorrect, correct enough, perfect</li>
</ul>

</Section>

<Section id="findings" label="Findings and future work">

<Findings
  items={[
    ["81%", "wanted main stations displayed on bus signs"],
    ["72.6%", "wanted occupancy information for the current bus"],
    ["84.2%", "wanted occupancy information for the next bus"],
    ["68.6%", "preferred the bus number on the left of the sign"],
    ["82.2%", "ranked Design 1 the clearest occupancy symbol"],
    ["3 s", "the scroll interval with the most reliable recall"],
  ]}
/>

<div class="prose">

Passengers valued simple, high-contrast cues confirming destination and occupancy
at a glance, above decorative clarity. Four design rules came out of it, each
traceable to an experiment rather than to taste.

</div>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Prioritise luminance contrast, not colour accents</li>
  <li>Use fonts that retain clarity under pixelation</li>
  <li>Keep motion predictable and minimise total scrolling content</li>
  <li>Keep decision-critical information static</li>
</ul>

<div class="prose">

Next: varied lighting and angles, other sign types such as liquid-crystal
displays, finer visual-acuity metrics, and how much a sign can carry before
clarity falls away.

</div>

</Section>
