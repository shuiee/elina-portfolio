<Section id="question" label="The question">

<p class="lede">{frontmatter.question}</p>

Six million people use London buses every day. I often saw passengers hesitate at
the stop, or board the wrong bus, and the sign was usually part of the reason.

The comparison that started it was Korean. Korean bus signage carries more
information in the same space and is easier to scan, and the reason is mechanical
rather than aesthetic: Korean characters need less horizontal space, so more fits at
the same legibility. That reframed the problem: London's signs are being
asked to carry an amount of information that the writing system cannot fit at a
readable size.

So the question was not which sign looks clearer. It was how much a person can
take in at once, and what a sign should do when it needs to say more than that.

A passenger who recalls "Shoredich" still boards the right bus. A binary grade
measures spelling accuracy, which is not what the sign is for. The sign has to be
read once, at distance, from a moving bus.

Grading on letters and their positions measures what actually matters: whether the
passenger could still act on what they read.
</CriterionBlock>

<p>The same principle set the terms for the two physical experiments. Rather than
asking which sign looked clearer, both were designed to produce numbers another
person could re-measure.</p>

</Section>

<Section id="method" label="Method">

<Method>

<Step n="01" title="Physical measurement at PEARL" when="UCL PEARL facility">
A printed sign and an LED sign, both showing **Shoreditch 388** — chosen for its
length and for mixing straight and curved letterforms.

Illuminance was sampled at eight fixed points, mirrored across both signs.
Luminance was sampled at fixed black and white points. Both were repeated at five
distances from 0.5 to 2.5 m.

Fixed, mirrored sampling points are what make the printed and the LED sign directly
comparable, and what make the run repeatable by somebody else.

<Figure ratio="16/9" caption="TODO(elina): PEARL experiment setup — printed sign and LED sign rigs, with the illuminance sampling grid marked." />
</Step>

<Step n="02" title="Font analysis in MATLAB">
`regionprops` for the area, eccentricity and circularity of every character.
`improfile` for pixel-intensity profiles drawn horizontally through the text,
showing how sharply brightness moves from background to letter edge and back.

Johnston 100 Medium — TfL's own face — was benchmarked against Optician Sans, which
is used in clinical visual-acuity testing, and against Arial, Times New Roman,
Motorway and Apple Chancery. The aim was not to declare a threshold, but to see
whether Johnston 100 behaves more like a clinical-clarity font or a decorative one
when you look only at geometry.

The output is a workflow rather than a verdict: a transport operator can run it on a
new font or a new sign and get the same kind of number back.

<Figure ratio="16/9" caption="TODO(elina): MATLAB output — grayscale, threshold and bounding-box stages, plus the intensity profile plots for printed and LED at close and far range." />
</Step>

<Step n="03" title="Passenger survey">
An online survey (Qualtrics) covering four questions: whether the current placement
of bus number and destination reads clearly; whether people want information the
signs do not currently carry; which occupancy symbol communicates fastest; and what
scrolling interval still allows recall.

The occupancy symbols tested were geometric rather than illustrative, because the
luminance work had already shown that high contrast and simple shapes are what
survive low light and a pixel grid.

<Section id="means" label="What it means">

<div class="prose">

Four implications came out of the three experiments together.

1. **Prioritise luminance contrast, not colour accents.** Contrast is the primary
   driver of readability, especially under bright ambient light. Changing colour does
   very little once contrast falls below a usable threshold.
2. **Use fonts that hold their clarity under pixelation.** Curved and decorative
   letterforms lose definition first on an LED matrix at distance. Geometric faces
   keep their edges.
3. **Keep motion predictable and minimise scrolling content.** Recall peaks at a
   3-second interval and drops with longer or less rhythmically consistent movement.
4. **Keep decision-critical information static.** Route number, destination, via
   stops and occupancy are what people act on, so they should stay in place.

**Still open:** testing under varied lighting and viewing angles; other display
types such as LCD; refining the visual-acuity metrics; and establishing how much
information can actually fit on a sign before clarity goes.

</div>
