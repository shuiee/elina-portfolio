<Stop label="Stop 01" title="Background">

In London, over six million people rely on buses every day, yet I often saw
passengers hesitating or boarding the wrong one because the signs were hard to
read. Coming from Korea, where bus signage is more information-rich and easier
to scan, this contrast stood out even more. It made me question why a city as
connected as London still struggles with such a fundamental part of wayfinding,
and how clearer visual systems could genuinely improve the passenger experience.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 02" title="Vision">

My objective was to explore how information is perceived in the transport
environment, and how better design choices, for example LED against printed signs
or using different orientations, can make bus signs clearer, friendlier, more
informative, and ultimately improve traveller experience.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 03" title="Problem framing" href="#framing">

A literature review into why passengers struggle with bus signs. Human vision
depends heavily on quick fixations and pre-attentive cues, and current signage
does not support that.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 04" title="Measuring luminance and illuminance" href="#light">

Turning readability into something measurable, under controlled conditions at
UCL's PEARL facility.

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 05" title="Evaluating fonts with MATLAB" href="#fonts">

Translating legibility into geometry and pixel data, to see whether Johnston 100

</Stop>

<Stop label="Stop 06" title="Passenger opinion" href="#survey">

<Section id="framing" label="Problem framing">

<div class="prose">

I conducted a literature review to understand why passengers struggle with bus
signs. Four failures came up repeatedly.

</div>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Unclear information hierarchy</li>
  <li>Inconsistent contrast and lighting</li>
  <li>Fonts that change clarity when digitised</li>
  <li>LED scrolling systems that can be difficult to read</li>
</ul>

<div class="prose">

Human vision depends heavily on quick fixations and pre-attentive cues, which
current signage does not support.

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="light" label="Measuring luminance and illuminance">

<div class="prose">

Turning readability into something measurable. I compared a real printed sign
against an LED sign under controlled conditions at UCL's PEARL facility, measuring
how much light each sign emits from its white letters and black background, and
how much light falls onto each sign.

The test word was settled before the test day: Shoreditch 388, chosen as a single
word of at least seven characters containing both straight and curved letters, so
edges and curves were tested under the same conditions.

</div>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Lighting passport to control ambient light and colour temperature</li>
  <li>8 fixed illuminance points, mirrored on the LED sign</li>
  <li>Luminance sampled on fixed black and white points</li>
  <li>Five distances, 0.5 to 2.5 m, repeated to smooth out hand-held noise</li>
  <li>Black cloth under the LED screen to reduce reflections</li>
</ul>

</Section>

<Section id="fonts" label="Evaluating fonts with MATLAB">

<div class="prose">

Translating legibility into geometry and pixel data. Using MATLAB's regionprops,
I calculated area, eccentricity and circularity for every character in Shoreditch
388, then drew a horizontal line through the text to extract a pixel-intensity
profile, which shows how brightness moves from a dark background to a bright
letter edge and back.

The aim was to remove subjectivity. Instead of saying one sign looks clearer, I
wanted a repeatable workflow a transport operator could run on a new font or a new
sign. Johnston 100 is benchmarked against Optician Sans, the face used in clinical
visual-acuity testing, so the comparison has a reference point outside transport.

</div>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Johnston 100 Medium</li>
  <li>Arial</li>
  <li>Times New Roman</li>
  <li>Motorway</li>
  <li>Apple Chancery</li>
  <li>Benchmarked against Optician Sans</li>
</ul>

<div class="prose">

Johnston 100 showed the most stable geometry under pixelation. Serif and
decorative fonts degraded quickly with distance on LED screens, and the
pixel-intensity profiles confirmed soft edges beyond about two metres.

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="survey" label="Passenger opinion">

<div class="prose">

Having quantified light and font behaviour, the last step was to find out how
people actually prefer information to be shown, and how much they can realistically
read from a scrolling LED sign. I sketched front and side sign concepts at the
angles passengers really see a double-decker from, then built a Qualtrics survey
around four questions: whether current placements are clear, whether people want
occupancy and via stations, which symbol communicates occupancy best, and what
scrolling interval allows a station name to be recalled.

Letters in English take up more horizontal space than Korean characters do, which
is a large part of why a Korean bus sign carries more information without losing
legibility.

</div>

<div class="prose">

If a passenger remembers "Shoredich" instead of "Shoreditch" they still comprehend
the sign and will almost certainly board the right bus, so a binary right or wrong
judgment would have thrown away the truth. I created fictitious station names,
assigned them to different scrolling intervals, and graded what participants typed
back against a marking scheme.

</div>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Intervals 1.5 s, 3 s, 4.5 s</li>
  <li>Threshold: 5 of 9 letters correct</li>
  <li>6 of 9 letters in the right position</li>
  <li>Graded incorrect, correct enough, perfect</li>
</ul>

</Section>

<Section id="findings" label="Findings and future work">

<Findings

    ["81%", "wanted main stations displayed on bus signs"],
    ["72.6%", "wanted occupancy information for the current bus"],
    ["84.2%", "wanted occupancy information for the next bus"],
    ["68.6%", "preferred the bus number on the left of the sign"],
    ["82.2%", "ranked Design 1 the clearest occupancy symbol"],
    ["3 s", "the scroll interval with the most reliable recall"],
  ]}
/>

<div class="prose">

Passengers valued simple, high-contrast cues confirming both destination and
occupancy at a glance, above decorative clarity. Four design rules came out of it,
each traceable to a specific experiment rather than to taste.

</div>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Prioritise luminance contrast, not colour accents</li>
  <li>Use fonts that retain clarity under pixelation</li>
  <li>Keep motion predictable and minimise total scrolling content</li>
  <li>Keep decision-critical information static</li>
</ul>

<div class="prose">

Looking ahead: testing under varied lighting and angles, assessing additional sign
types such as liquid-crystal displays, refining the visual-acuity metrics, and
studying how much information a sign can realistically carry before clarity starts
to fall away.

</div>

</Section>
