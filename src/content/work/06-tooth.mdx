<Section id="question" label="The question">

<p class="lede">{frontmatter.question}</p>

Enamel is the only tissue in the human body that cannot grow back once it is lost.
Whatever happens to it stays happened. That makes a tooth a record of two things at
once: the person it belonged to, and the world they were living in.

Six European datasets, one composite tooth, playing from the start of the common era
to 1900.

</Section>

<Section id="piece" label="The prototype">

<Figure
  src="/images/tooth/radial.jpg"
  ratio="1700/901"
  caption="The radial timeline. Each of the five records leaves the centre as its own line, a hollow circle marks the year that record begins, and the line darkens over the years it actually covers, so a gap in the evidence shows as a pale stretch."
/>

<div class="prose">

A jaw engraving inks itself in and swings open. A first molar and a canine turn to face
the reader, a plane cuts them open, and then every kind of record arrives on them in
time order: decay, wear, childhood stress lines, lead in the enamel, and disease DNA
rising from the root into the pulp.

One decision carries that figure. Distance from the centre is how long ago a year is, on
a square-root scale shared by every line, so seventeen centuries of pathogen data and six
thousand years of metals fit in one picture without either one vanishing.

Everything after the opening is set like a scientific journal: running heads, numbered
plates and figures, notes rather than captions. The format is the argument. It asks to be
read as evidence, so it is laid out as evidence.

[Open the live prototype](https://shuiee.github.io/tooth-untold-prototype/)

</div>

</Section>

Grouping the sources by subject turned out to be the wrong cut. Grouping them by how the
evidence was made is what decided how much weight each layer could carry.

</div>

<ul class="tags">
  <li>Primary standardised osteology</li>
  <li>Aggregated primary molecular data</li>
  <li>Literature transcription</li>
  <li>Survey and administrative records</li>

<div class="prose">

Only the first was measured directly and consistently: one codebook, trained observers,
excavated skeletons. The pathogen layer is ninety sampling decisions pooled through a
public archive, not one. The metals layer is values read out of published papers, with no
new excavation and no new assay. The intervention layer is living volunteers examined and
claim forms counted, which is a different thing again: bone, then body, then bureaucracy.

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="checking" label="Checking every number">

<div class="prose">

We used AI to pull figures out of papers. The useful question was never whether to use
it, but what would have to be true before anything it produced could go on screen. Four
rules, applied every time.

- We never made up a number. If it was not in a paper, it is not on screen.
- We checked the paper, not the spreadsheet. The AI's version was never the final word.
- We referenced where the numbers came from. Every chart names its paper or dataset.
- We did not let the AI mark its own work. When it said this is right, we checked that too.

The rules were worth having because each layer had its own way of going quietly wrong.

</div>

<Section id="limits" label="What the data cannot say">

<div class="prose">

Nobody excavated Europe at random. Every site in the osteology module exists because
somebody had a reason to dig there: a grant, a motorway, a cathedral restoration.
Three of its seventeen countries hold 48% of the individuals, Lithuania alone outweighs
Spain, Portugal, Greece, Hungary and Romania combined, and six European countries are not
in it at all. The sources also disagree about when an era began and ended, so the
periods both overlap and leave holes.

The one that mattered most to the story is the one we put on a slide of its own. The
industrial tail is the thinnest part of every layer: 796 adults across 7 sites and 4
countries, and 10 human pathogen samples. The narrative peaks exactly where the evidence
runs out.

So the tooth on screen is an abstraction, not a reconstruction. It is drawn to carry
trends and relationships, and the page says where it is uncertain rather than smoothing
it over.

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="chosen" label="The record we choose">

<div class="prose">

Everything above happened to people. Intervention is the first part of the dental record
that is deliberately chosen: scheduled, paid for, consented to, and usually permanent. A
filling, a crown, a veneer all overwrite the evidence underneath them.

That inverts the project. Modern teeth may say less about what the world did to a person
and more about what they chose to change, which makes them a record of access and income
as much as of diet and disease.

</div>

The figure underneath it is the one that stayed with me. 24.1% of industrial adults
reached death with no caries at all. A modern adult with no visible decay might have
three or four fillings by thirty.

</div>

<Section id="process" label="Process and contribution">

<div class="prose">

A three-person studio team: Lily, an industrial designer, Ali, in UX and product
management, and me, coming in from civil engineering. We consulted Dr Peter Ungar, a
dental anthropologist at the University of Arkansas, on what the record can and cannot
be asked to show.

My work was the tooth itself and the layer built on it. The teeth are signed distance
fields ray-marched in a fragment shader, so decay, wear, stress-line grooves and enamel
lead are carved into the geometry rather than drawn over it, and a JavaScript copy of the
same distance function places every mark exactly on the surface the shader draws. I built
the opening sequence, the pathogen section, the responsive behaviour and the automatic
publishing, and integrated the team's sections into the single piece.

<Section id="reflections" label="Reflections">

<div class="prose">

The part I would carry into any project is the rule that nothing goes on screen unless it
traces to a row in a paper. It sounds like a research rule. It is really a design rule,
because it decides what the picture is allowed to say, and I have now written one twice:
once as a marking scheme for how much of a bus sign a passenger can recall, and once as a
provenance check on what a model pulled out of a journal article.

Working with AI on research made the gap between fast and finished very wide. Getting a
number out of a paper took seconds. Establishing that it was the right number took the
afternoon, and the afternoon is the part that made the piece defensible.

The hardest thing to accept was putting our thinnest evidence at the loudest moment of
the story. The industrial period is where the argument lands and where the data almost
runs out, and the honest move was to keep the peak and say so on the page rather than
quietly choosing a different ending.

</div>
