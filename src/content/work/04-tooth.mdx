<Section id="question" label="The question">

<p class="lede">{frontmatter.question}</p>

Enamel is the only tissue in the human body that cannot grow back once it is lost.
Whatever happens to it stays happened.

That makes a tooth a record of two things at once: the person it belonged to, and the
world they were living in.

Six European datasets, one composite tooth, playing from the start of the common era
to 1900.

</Section>

<div class="prose">

A jaw engraving inks itself in and swings open. A first molar and a canine turn to face
the reader, and a plane cuts them open.

Then every kind of record arrives on them in time order: decay, wear, childhood stress
lines, lead in the enamel, and disease DNA rising from the root into the pulp.

One decision carries that figure. Distance from the centre is how long ago a year is, on
a square-root scale shared by every line, so seventeen centuries of pathogen data and six
thousand years of metals fit in one picture without either one vanishing.

Everything after the opening is set like a scientific journal: running heads, numbered
plates and figures, notes rather than captions. It asks to be read as evidence, so it is
laid out as evidence.

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="checking" label="Checking every number">

<div class="prose">

We used AI to pull figures out of papers. The useful question was not whether to use
it, but what had to be true before anything it produced could go on screen.

If a number was not in a paper, it is not on screen.

<Section id="limits" label="What the data cannot say">

<div class="prose">

Nobody excavated Europe at random. Every site in the osteology module exists because
somebody had a reason to dig there: a grant, a motorway, a cathedral restoration.

Three of its seventeen countries hold 48% of the individuals, Lithuania alone outweighs
Spain, Portugal, Greece, Hungary and Romania combined, and six European countries are not
in it at all.

The sources also disagree about when an era began and ended, so the periods overlap and
leave holes.

The one that mattered most got a slide of its own. The industrial tail is the thinnest
part of every layer: 796 adults across 7 sites and 4 countries, and 10 human pathogen
samples.

The narrative peaks exactly where the evidence runs out.

So the tooth on screen is an abstraction, not a reconstruction. It is drawn to carry
trends and relationships, and the page says where it is uncertain rather than smoothing
it over.

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="chosen" label="The record we choose">

<div class="prose">

Everything above happened to people. Intervention is the first part of the dental record
that is deliberately chosen: scheduled, paid for, consented to, and usually permanent.

A filling, a crown, a veneer all overwrite the evidence underneath them.

That inverts the project. Modern teeth may say less about what the world did to a person
and more about what they chose to change, which makes them a record of access and income
as much as of diet and disease.

</div>

<Section id="reflections" label="Reflections">

<div class="prose">

The rule I would carry into any project is that nothing goes on screen unless it traces
to a row in a paper. It sounds like a research rule.

It is a design rule, because it decides what the picture is allowed to say, and I have
now written one twice: once as a marking scheme for how much of a bus sign a passenger
can recall, once as a provenance check on what a model pulled out of a journal.

Working with AI on research made the gap between fast and finished very wide. Getting a
number out of a paper took seconds.

Establishing that it was the right number took the afternoon, and the afternoon is the
part that made the piece defensible.

The hardest thing to accept was putting our thinnest evidence at the loudest moment of
the story. The industrial period is where the argument lands and where the data almost
runs out.

The honest move was to keep the peak and say so, rather than quietly choosing a different
ending.

</div>

</Section>
