<Section id="question" label="The question">

<p class="lede">{frontmatter.question}</p>

Patchwork is a slow platform for sharing cultural fragments — the small, specific
things people notice about where they live and where they are from. The design
problem is that the mechanisms that make sharing feel rewarding are often the same
ones that make it feel like performance.

So the question is not how to remove social reward, but which rewards can be kept
without the pressure that usually comes with them.

<Section id="method" label="Method">

<Method>

<Step n="01" title="Competitor analysis">
Seven platforms that overlap with what Patchwork cares about — slow sharing, small
circles, documenting everyday moments — read against the five features above rather
than against their positioning.
</Step>

<Step n="02" title="User interviews, and then the same interviews again">
The second round is the part of the project I would point to first.

Round one produced clear themes: connection, sharing habits, fatigue with existing
platforms. It was usable data, but it pulled the project toward the behaviours people
already have, and those behaviours belong to the platforms Patchwork is a response to.

Rather than treat the findings as requirements, I re-analysed the same data against
what the project set out to do, looking for tensions inside existing behaviour rather
than for confirmation of it.

The exhibit that came out of it is in [what it showed](#showed).

<Figure ratio="16/9" caption="TODO(elina): affinity diagram from round 1 alongside the round 2 thematic grouping." />
</Step>

<Step n="03" title="A rough MVP, built out of sequence">
I built a working MVP before formalising UX strategy or wireframes — early, and
deliberately out of order — so the team had something concrete to disagree with
instead of a document.

It was vibe-coded rather than hand-built. As a way of testing whether a feature set
holds together in context that was a reasonable trade, but it is not evidence of
front-end craft and I am not presenting it as such.
</Step>

<Step n="04" title="Narrowing scope">
Evaluating the MVP against the reframed analysis made it possible to say which
directions risked reproducing existing social platforms. Scope narrowed to features
that only make sense if the goal is intentional use: lightweight save-and-revisit,
optional structure around posting, and limits that make passive scrolling
unrewarding.
</Step>

</Method>

<div class="prose">

The primary user is 20–35, oriented around 소확행 — small but certain happiness. Less a
demographic than a posture: people who want to keep a record of something specific
and unspectacular, and who currently do not, because the places available for it tend
to turn it into a performance.

Two things came out of the second pass that the first pass could not have produced:

- The fatigue is with the reward mechanism rather than with frequency. People are not
  asking to post less; they are asking for posting not to be scored.
- The hesitation to document a meaningful moment is about who will see it and how
  fast it will be consumed — not about the effort of making it.

<Section id="means" label="What it means">

<div class="prose">

The transferable part is not the product. It is that interview findings describe the
present, and a project whose purpose is to differ from the present cannot take them
straight as requirements. Round one was not wrong; it was answering a different
question from the one the project was asking.

**Honest status:** Patchwork is at MVP-draft stage. Nothing has shipped to users, and
there are no usage numbers to report. What exists is a feature scope with reasons
attached, and a draft build.

**Next:** UX strategy and wireframes against the narrowed scope, then testing whether
the constraints that are supposed to reduce pressure actually do.

</div>
